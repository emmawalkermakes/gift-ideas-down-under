Wrapping is the last job before Christmas, and it's usually done in a hurry. Choosing your wrapping approach a few weeks early means you can buy (or save) the right materials once, instead of making three trips for tape.

Here is how the common options compare, followed by notes on which suits which gift.

## The comparison at a glance

| Option | Effort | Looks | Reusable? | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Printed wrapping paper | Medium | Festive, colourful | Rarely | Boxes, kids' gifts | Foil, glitter and laminated papers may not be recyclable |
| Plain kraft (brown) paper | Medium | Simple, easy to dress up | Sometimes | Everything; personalising with stamps or handwriting | Plain on its own, so add twine or a tag |
| Fabric wrap (furoshiki-style) | Low once learned | Soft, premium | Yes | Awkward shapes, bottles, books | You need a square of fabric big enough |
| Tea towel or scarf as wrap | Low | Practical | It *is* part of the gift | Kitchen and home gifts | Matching the fabric to the person |
| Gift bags | Very low | Neat | Yes, often | Soft or odd-shaped gifts, last-minute | Can look generic; tissue helps |
| Boxes and tins | Low | Tidy | Yes | Baking, small items, fragile things | Bulk if you're posting |
| Reused paper (maps, sheet music, kids' art, newspaper) | Medium | Charming, one-off | Sometimes | Personal gifts, smaller items | Newsprint ink can rub off on light surfaces |

## Printed wrapping paper

The classic. It's the quickest way to make a pile of gifts look like Christmas, and children generally love a bright roll.

**Pros:** easy to find, lots of designs, folds crisply around boxes.

**Cons:** most of it gets torn and binned on the day. Recyclability varies with the type of paper and where you live. Papers with foil, glitter or a plastic coating are the ones most often excluded, so check your local council's guidance on what can go in your recycling bin. A common rule of thumb is the "scrunch test": if the paper stays scrunched in a ball, it's more likely to be plain paper; if it springs back, it may contain plastic.

**Best for:** boxed gifts and anything for younger kids.

Brown paper is the most flexible option on the list. One roll works for every person and every gift, and it becomes "personalised" with almost no effort: write the recipient's name in marker, use a rubber stamp, add a sprig of rosemary or a gum leaf, or tie it with twine.

**Pros:** looks good on its own or decorated, often easier to recycle than coated paper, works for posting.

**Cons:** can look plain if you skip the finishing touches.

**Best for:** wrapping lots of gifts consistently, and anyone who likes writing names straight onto the parcel instead of using tags.

## Fabric wrapping

<figcaption>Photo: <a href="https://www.pexels.com/photo/person-holding-an-eco-friendly-gift-wrap-6305932/">Anastasia Shuraeva</a> on Pexels</figcaption>
</figure>

Furoshiki is a Japanese tradition of wrapping and carrying things in a square of cloth, and the basic knots are easy to learn from a diagram. Fabric wrap is excellent for shapes paper hates: bottles, round items, soft toys.

**Pros:** no tape, quick once you know two or three knots, reusable for years.

**Cons:** you need square fabric of the right size. If the wrap is meant to come back to you, say so kindly; otherwise assume it's part of the gift.

**A practical variation:** use a tea towel, napkin or scarf as the wrap. The wrapping becomes a second gift, which suits a cook, a host or a friend who's trying to cut waste.

## Gift bags, boxes and tins

These win on speed. A bag with a sheet of tissue paper over the top takes seconds and handles odd shapes that would take ten minutes to wrap.

Boxes and tins are the best choice for fragile items and home baking. A tin of biscuits needs nothing more than a ribbon and a tag.

**Pros:** fast, reusable, protective.

**Cons:** bags can look generic, and bulky boxes cost more to post. Re-gifted bags are perfectly fine, but check for an old tag inside first.

Old road maps, sheet music, pages from a damaged book, children's paintings and the weekend newspaper all make memorable wrapping. A map of a place the recipient loves is especially good for a personal gift.

**Pros:** costs nothing, often more meaningful than bought paper.

**Cons:** sizes are limited, and newspaper ink can transfer onto light-coloured items or hands.

## Tips for wrapping in a Brisbane summer

- **Humidity affects tape.** On sticky days, some tapes lift from paper. Double-sided tape on the inside fold holds better, and fabric wraps avoid tape altogether.
- **Keep wrapped chocolate and candles out of the heat.** Don't leave them in the car or under a sunny window. Wrap them last, or put them in the fridge with a note.
- **Wrap and label as you go.** Writing the name on the gift while you still remember what's inside saves a frantic "which one was Dad's?" moment on Christmas Eve.
- **For posting, wrap inside the box.** Decorative wrapping goes inside a sturdy postal box with padding, never on the outside. The [Christmas posting timeline](/posts/christmas-gift-posting-timeline-australia-2026/) has the cut-off dates.

## Which should you choose?

- **Lots of gifts, small budget:** one big roll of kraft paper and some twine.
- **Kids:** printed paper they can tear into.
- **Bottles, books, odd shapes:** fabric or a gift bag.
- **Home baking or fragile pieces:** tins or boxes.
- **Someone sentimental:** reused paper with a story, like a map of their home town.

There's no wrong answer. A mix of two methods covers most families' Christmas pile neatly.
