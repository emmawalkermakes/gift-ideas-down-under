Ask a teenager what they want for Christmas and you'll usually get one of three answers: a shrug, "I don't know", or something wildly beyond the budget.

I have a teenager in the house, and I've learned that the shrug doesn't mean they don't care. It often means they don't want to commit to something that might come out wrong, or they genuinely haven't thought about it. So the job falls to us — parents, aunties, uncles, grandparents, godparents — to work it out.

Here's how I approach it, and the ideas that have landed well (and a few that haven't).

## Do some quiet detective work first

Before you open a single shopping site, spend a week paying attention. Teens drop more hints than they realise.

- **What do they use until it falls apart?** A worn-out pair of headphones, a phone case held together with tape, a school bag with a broken zip. Replacing a favourite with a better version is one of the safest gifts there is.
- **What do they talk about with friends?** A band, a game, a show, a sports team, a creator they watch. Listen in on car trips — it's where teens talk most.
- **What's on their bedroom walls or desk?** Posters, photos, plants, collections, art supplies.
- **Ask a sibling or a cousin.** They often know exactly what's on the wish list.
- **Ask a sideways question.** "If you had a free Saturday and some money, what would you do?" usually gets a more useful answer than "What do you want for Christmas?"

And if you're not their parent, it's perfectly fine to text the parent. Most will be relieved you asked.

## Ideas by type of teen

These aren't boxes every teenager fits neatly into, but they're a handy starting point.

### The one who's always got earbuds in

Music, podcasts, audiobooks — this teen lives with sound.

- A gift card for the streaming or audiobook service they already use (check which one first)
- A protective case for their earbuds or headphones, especially if theirs is battered
- Tickets to a gig, if an artist they love is touring nearby — check the venue's age rules before you buy
- A small portable speaker for the beach or backyard

### The creative one

Drawing, writing, making videos, crafting, fashion.

- Good-quality materials in the medium they already use: a set of decent pens or markers, a sketchbook with proper paper, a refill of whatever they burn through fastest
- A class or workshop — pottery, life drawing, sewing, photography — ideally one they can do with a friend
- A personalised sketchbook or journal with their name or initials on the cover. A small touch that makes it feel like *theirs*
- A desk lamp, if they're working late with poor light

Ask the teen or their art teacher what they actually use. Creative kids often have strong opinions on brands, and a giant all-in-one set from the toy aisle can miss the mark.

### The sporty one

- Gear that replaces something worn out: a drink bottle, a training bag, socks (yes, really — good sports socks get used)
- Tickets to a game: AFL, NRL, cricket, A-League, netball, whatever their team plays
- A voucher for a surf lesson, rock climbing session or a few visits to an indoor trampoline or bouldering centre
- Sun protection for summer sport: a good hat, polarised sunnies, a sweat-resistant sunscreen

### The gamer

Gaming gifts are tricky, because what works depends entirely on the console or platform they use, and whether they already own something.

- A gift card for the store on their platform is the safest choice
- Accessories: a controller charging dock, a headset stand, a desk mat
- Merchandise from a favourite game — a hoodie, a figure, an art book
- If you're unsure, ask: "Which console or PC store should a gift card be for?"

Check the classification on any game before buying. In Australia, games are classified by the Australian Classification Board, and a game's MA15+ or R18+ rating matters, especially for younger teens.

### The one who's into skincare, hair and getting ready

- A gift card for the store or brand they already use, so they can choose
- A nice toiletry bag, hair accessories or a makeup bag with their initial
- A mirror with good light for their desk or dressing table

Leave specific skincare products to them unless you know exactly what they use. Teens' skin can be sensitive, and many are particular about ingredients.

### The reader

- A book from a series they're halfway through (check which ones they've got)
- A bookshop gift card plus a trip to the bookshop together
- A personalised bookmark, bookplate stamp or embossed library-style stamp for their own collection
- A reading light for late nights

### The "I really need nothing" teen

Some teenagers genuinely don't want more stuff. For them:

- **Experiences**: a day trip, a concert, a cooking class, a movie and dinner just with you
- **Money in a thoughtful form**: cash in a card with a note about what you hope they'll do with it, or a contribution towards something they're saving for
- **Practical upgrades** they'd never buy themselves: a better pillow, a quality water bottle, a phone charger long enough to reach the bed

## Gifts I'd skip (or check first)

- **Anything with a fixed opinion attached**: "motivational" books, diet or fitness gadgets, or clothes to change how they dress. They tend to land badly.
- **Clothes without a size and style check.** Teen sizing and taste change quickly. A gift receipt helps a lot.
- **Joke gifts that hit a sore spot**, especially around appearance or school.
- **Novelty items for a younger age group.** Nothing stings a 14-year-old quite like a gift aimed at a 10-year-old.
- **Big surprises involving their phone or social media** — new accounts, devices or apps — without checking with their parent first.

## A word on personalised gifts for teens

Personalised gifts can be brilliant or a bit cringe, and the difference is usually subtlety. A full name in big curly script on a pink mug? Probably a miss for most teens. Initials in a clean font on a phone case, journal, drink bottle or bag tag? Much more likely to be used.

A good test: would they be happy for their friends to see it? If yes, personalise away.

## Last thing: the card matters more than you think

A teenager may not say much on Christmas morning, but many of them keep cards. A few lines telling them something specific you've noticed this year — how hard they've worked at something, how they looked after a sibling, how much you've enjoyed a laugh with them — can mean more than the gift itself.

Short, real, and not embarrassing is the brief. They'll pretend not to care, and they'll probably keep it in a drawer for years.
