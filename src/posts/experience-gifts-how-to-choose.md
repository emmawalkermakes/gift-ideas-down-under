An experience gift — a class, a day out, tickets, a meal — is a lovely idea on paper. No clutter, a memory instead of an object, and often something the person would never book for themselves.

The catch is that experience gifts have a habit of going unused. The voucher sits on the fridge, life gets busy, and suddenly it's eighteen months later. The good news is that most of the reasons they fail are predictable, and you can design around them before you buy.

## Five questions to ask before you buy

### 1. Would they choose this themselves?

An experience you'd love isn't automatically one they'll love. A hot-air balloon flight is a dream for some people and a nightmare for anyone afraid of heights. The safest experiences are an upgraded version of something they already enjoy: a cooking class for the person who's always in the kitchen, a guided walk for the bushwalker, a better seat at a show by an artist they already listen to.

If you're unsure, ask a partner, housemate or close friend. Or, if surprise doesn't matter much, ask the person directly: "If you had a free Saturday and someone else was paying, what would you do?"

### 2. Can they actually get there?

Location is the quiet killer of experience vouchers. Before buying, check:

- How far is it from where they live, and how would they get there?
- Is it only available on weekdays, or at times they work?
- Does it suit their mobility and health?
- If it's for interstate family, is the provider genuinely near *them*?

### 3. How flexible is the booking?

Look at the provider's terms before paying:

- **Is there an expiry date?** Most gift vouchers sold in Australia must now be valid for at least three years (see our [gift card guide](/posts/how-to-give-a-gift-card-that-feels-personal/) for the details and exceptions), but tickets for a specific date are a different thing entirely.
- **Can bookings be moved?** Outdoor experiences in a Queensland summer may be affected by heat, storms or rain. Find out what happens if the weather turns.
- **Is it weather-dependent at all?** An indoor class in January is often easier to enjoy than a midday outdoor activity.

### 4. Does it need company?

Some experiences are better shared, and some are only possible with another person (a tandem kayak, a cooking class for two). If the gift implies "you and someone else", think about who that someone is. Giving a "for two" experience and coming along yourself can make a beautiful gift for a parent or friend. Giving one to a single friend who'd have to find a companion can feel like homework.

### 5. Who does the booking?

This is the step most people skip, and it's the main reason vouchers don't get used. If the recipient has to call, check availability, coordinate with others and lock in a date, it can easily slide down their list. If you can, book a date together on Christmas Day or in the week after, while the excitement is fresh.

## Ideas by personality

Rather than a long list of activities, it's more useful to start with the person:

**The maker or hobbyist.** A one-off workshop in something next to their current hobby: a pottery or ceramics class for someone who paints, a natural dyeing workshop for a knitter, a beginner woodworking session for someone who loves fixing things. Small studios often fill their January holiday classes early.

**The food lover.** A cooking class in a cuisine they love, a long lunch somewhere they've mentioned, a market tour, or a coffee or chocolate tasting. A booked table with the date already chosen is often more exciting than an open voucher.

**The outdoorsy one.** A guided kayak or stand-up paddleboard session, a snorkelling trip, a national park camping weekend (some campsites need booking well ahead in the holidays), or a beginner rock-climbing class at an indoor gym for the hot months.

**The culture fan.** Tickets to a show, a gallery's paid exhibition, a comedy night or a concert. Check the date suits them before buying, or give the tickets with a "let's pick a show together" note instead.

**The one who needs a rest.** A massage, a day spa, or simply a booked afternoon off with someone else handling the kids, pets or chores. A handmade voucher for "a day where I take care of everything" can mean more than anything you could buy.

**The family.** One shared outing beats a pile of individual presents: a day at a wildlife park, a family pass for a local attraction, or a picnic day out you plan and pack.

## Make it real on Christmas morning

An experience is invisible on the day, which can make it feel flat next to wrapped presents. A few ways to fix that:

- **Wrap a clue.** A wooden spoon for the cooking class, a pair of swimming goggles for the snorkelling trip, a small paint palette for the art workshop.
- **Print or write a proper card** with the what, where and when. If the date's already booked, put it in big letters.
- **Add a small physical gift** they can use on the day, such as a water bottle, a hat or a good notebook.
- **Put it in the calendar together** before the end of Christmas Day.

## A short checklist

Before you pay for an experience, you should be able to answer yes to most of these:

- They'd plausibly choose this for themselves.
- It's somewhere they can easily get to, at a time they're free.
- You've read the expiry, rescheduling and weather policies.
- You know who's going with them (if anyone).
- There's a plan for when it will actually be booked.

Get those right, and an experience gift is genuinely hard to beat. It's the kind of present people still bring up years later: "Remember that class you gave me?"
