Being invited to someone else's Christmas lunch is lovely, and it comes with one recurring worry: what do I bring? Here are the questions people tend to ask, with honest answers for an Australian summer Christmas.

## Do I need to bring anything at all?

Yes, almost always. Even if the host says "just bring yourself", arriving empty-handed can feel awkward on Christmas Day specifically. The trick is to bring something that adds to the day without creating work for the host.

That means the best contribution is usually one of two things: the food or drink they have actually asked for, or a small gift that does not need to be dealt with on the day.

## What's the first thing I should do?

Ask. A short message a week or two out is perfectly normal:

> "Looking forward to the 25th! What can I bring? Happy to do a salad, dessert, drinks or ice."

Offering a few specific options makes it easier for the host to say yes to one. Many hosts have a menu planned and would rather you didn't turn up with a surprise trifle when there are already two.

Also ask, or check quietly with someone else, about dietary needs and any allergies at the table. If you are bringing food, label it (even a sticky note saying "contains nuts" helps).

## The host said "nothing, honestly". Now what?

Believe them about the food, and bring a small gift instead. Good options are ones the host can put aside and enjoy later:

- Something for after Christmas: good coffee, tea, or a breakfast treat for Boxing Day.
- A bottle of something (if they drink) that is clearly *for them*, not for the table. Say so: "This one is for you, not for today."
- A candle, hand cream or soap for the bathroom, which will get used.
- A plant that can go straight into the garden or onto a balcony.
- Something handmade or locally made: a small ceramic dish, a tea towel, a jar of chutney from a market stall.

A small personal touch goes a long way. A gift tag with their name, or a card with a few real lines in it, makes even a modest gift feel considered.

## Is wine still a good host gift?

It can be, with two caveats. First, not everyone drinks, so make sure you know the host does. Second, don't expect it to be opened at lunch. A bottle given as a gift belongs to the host, to drink whenever they like.

If you are bringing drinks for the table, that is a separate contribution, and it helps to bring them already chilled.

## What should I bring if it's hot?

Think about the trip, not just the dish. A Queensland Christmas Day can be hot and humid, and food spends time in a car before it reaches the table.

- **Keep cold food cold.** Transport salads, desserts, seafood and anything with dairy or mayonnaise in an esky with ice bricks, and ask the host before you arrive whether there's fridge space or whether you should keep it in the esky.
- **Choose dishes that cope with warmth.** Many grain, roast vegetable and leafy salads hold up better than creamy ones. Add dressings at the table.
- **Don't add to the host's fridge problem.** Space is tight on Christmas Day. Bringing your own ice, or a bag of ice for the drinks tub, is often more welcome than a dish.
- **Chocolate and heat don't mix.** If you're bringing chocolates as a gift, keep them out of the hot car and hand them over to go straight into the fridge.

For food safety specifics, Food Standards Australia New Zealand and Queensland Health both publish guidance on keeping food safe in hot weather. It's worth a quick read if you're bringing anything perishable.

## What are some useful contributions people forget?

The unglamorous ones are often the most appreciated:

- Ice. Lots of ice.
- A second esky or drinks tub.
- Extra chairs or a folding table, if you have them and the host is short.
- Containers for leftovers, so the host isn't giving away their good containers.
- Sunscreen and insect repellent if lunch is outdoors.
- Help with the washing up, offered sincerely and early.

## Do I need to bring gifts for everyone there?

Usually not. Ask the host how presents are being handled. Many families do a Kris Kringle or Secret Santa so that guests only buy for one person, and some keep gift-giving to the immediate family. If a Kris Kringle is planned, our [Kris Kringle ideas under $20](/posts/kris-kringle-gift-ideas-under-20-australia/) might help.

If children will be there and you are close to the family, a small something for each child is a kind gesture, but check with the parents first so nobody ends up with a much bigger pile than their siblings or cousins.

## What if I'm a guest at a "blended" Christmas for the first time?

Meeting a partner's family, or joining friends' family celebrations, is common at Christmas. A few simple principles:

- Ask your contact person (the friend or partner) what is normal in that family, including whether gifts are expected.
- Bring a host gift for the person cooking, even if someone else invited you.
- Keep it modest. An expensive gift can feel like pressure.
- A handwritten card thanking them for including you is never wrong.

## Should I follow up afterwards?

A short thank-you message the next day is a nice finish. Mention one specific thing (the pavlova, the cricket in the backyard, the new board game). If you borrowed containers or left something behind, organise its return quickly. Hosts tend to remember the guests who made the day easier.

## The quick answer

Ask the host what they need, bring that (cold, labelled and in an esky), add a small gift they can enjoy later, and offer to help with the clean-up. That covers almost every Christmas lunch.
